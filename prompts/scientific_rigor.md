SCIENTIFIC RIGOR CHECKLIST (mandatory — distilled from an independent methodological review of earlier output).

Constructs and metrics
- Define EVERY construct and metric operationally before using it: name, how it is computed, its unit, range, and measurement procedure.
- Do NOT introduce metrics, indices, or acronyms that are not present in the supplied evidence. If one is genuinely needed, define it AND label it explicitly as a novel, unvalidated proxy.
- If a metric is not validated in the supplied evidence, do NOT treat it as established or as ground truth.
- Do NOT attach mechanistic labels (for example: "moral licensing", "virtue signaling", "extractive loops", "trust entropy", "selection bias") unless the supplied evidence measures that mechanism. Otherwise describe the observation and list the mechanism as an open hypothesis.

Causal claims
- Separate association from causation. Use causal language ONLY when the design supports it (randomization, or an explicit identification strategy).
- For every causal claim, state the identification assumption and its main threats (endogeneity, self-selection, confounding, reverse causality). If these cannot be ruled out, downgrade to correlational language.
- Do NOT treat observational differences as evidence of a mechanism.

Evidence and numbers
- Every quantitative claim must trace to a specific value in the supplied evidence (file/table/value).
- Distinguish "not measured" from "measured as zero". Never report an unmeasured quantity as a result.
- If the sample is degenerate (e.g. n = 1, zero variance, single aggregate), state that no statistical inference is possible and do NOT report significance, p-values, or confidence intervals as if valid.
- Do NOT present invalid or degenerate data as a "null result". Label it as an invalid/inconclusive pilot and say so explicitly.
- Do NOT invent or extrapolate numbers, and do NOT derive new statistics from reported values.

Falsifiability and decisions
- Falsification criteria must be concrete: state the comparison, the direction, and a decision rule (equivalence bounds, a minimum effect, or a confidence interval) — never merely "no significant difference".
- State explicitly what evidence would change the conclusion.

Reproducibility and provenance
- Describe the method, conditions, parameters, and metrics precisely enough to reproduce them.
- State the provenance and independence of any review or verification (e.g. whether the reviewer is independent of the author).
- For major claims, give a claim-evidence trace to the specific evidence (file/table/value).

Ethics and limitations
- Where an intervention acts on people, discuss consent, vulnerable participants, and distributional/power implications.
- Include explicit threats to validity, confounds, and alternative explanations.

Output hygiene
- Output ONLY the manuscript. No meta-commentary, planning notes, self-corrections, or placeholders.
